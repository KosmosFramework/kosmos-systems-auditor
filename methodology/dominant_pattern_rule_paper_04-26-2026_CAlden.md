## Abstract

Systems auditing frameworks designed to evaluate socioeconomic institutions face a persistent methodological challenge: the tendency to inflate performance scores for extractive systems by crediting scattered positive outcomes that flow selectively to privileged populations. This paper identifies the theoretical source of this distortion and proposes a formal methodological correction through the Dominant Pattern Priority Rule. We demonstrate that in extractive systems, selective benefit distribution constitutes the mechanism of extraction rather than evidence against it, requiring fundamental revision of scoring protocols. The refined methodology prevents both upward score inflation in extractive systems and downward score deflation in regenerative systems by ensuring that audit outputs accurately represent what systems optimize for rather than averaging across deliberately unequal participant experiences. We validate this approach through application to the Texas Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program, demonstrating how the rule resolves scoring ambiguities while providing operational clarity for automated audit implementation. The Dominant Pattern Priority Rule represents a critical refinement to adversarial systems analysis methodology, with implications for artificial intelligence training constraint mitigation and rigorous institutional evaluation.

**Keywords:** systems auditing, extraction mechanisms, methodological bias, institutional analysis, dominant pattern analysis, adversarial methodology, AI training constraints

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The Challenge of Institutional Evaluation

Evaluating complex socioeconomic systems requires frameworks capable of distinguishing between institutions that genuinely serve stated purposes and those that deploy stated purposes as rhetorical cover for extractive operations. Traditional performance metrics often obscure this distinction by treating all positive outcomes as evidence of system functionality regardless of their distribution across affected populations. When a food assistance program enrolls seventy percent of eligible participants while systematically excluding thirty percent through procedural barriers, conventional analysis may characterize this as partial success requiring incremental improvement. A more rigorous analysis must ask whether the exclusion of thirty percent represents capacity limitation or architectural design.

The KOSMOS systems auditing framework employs Fundamental Design Principles derived from natural systems to evaluate whether human institutions exhibit symbiotic, hybrid, or extractive operational patterns. This methodology generates numerical scores across eight dimensions including Symbiotic Purpose, Reciprocal Ethics, Distributed Agency, and Adaptive Resilience. During application of this framework to governance systems, we identified a persistent analytical distortion: the tendency to inflate scores for extractive systems by crediting positive outcomes that flow selectively to privileged populations, treating these outcomes as evidence of partial system success rather than as the selective distribution mechanism through which extraction operates.

### 1.2 The Texas SNAP Case Study

Application of the KOSMOS framework to the Texas Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program revealed this distortion with particular clarity. Initial scoring calculations generated a Symbiotic Purpose score of 2.0 out of 10, reflecting the program's exclusion of thirty percent of eligible Texans and maintenance of food insecurity rates of 17.6 percent overall and 22 percent for children. However, during the scoring process, repeated impulses arose to increase this score to 2.5 or higher based on the observation that the program does deliver benefits to 3.47 million people.

This impulse represented a fundamental analytical error. The seventy percent enrollment rate does not mitigate the extractive architecture that excludes thirty percent; it demonstrates that the exclusion architecture contains calibrated gaps permitting passage for populations possessing specific advantages including English language proficiency, digital access, housing stability, documentation availability, and time resources to sustain months-long application processes. The positive outcome experienced by the seventy percent is not evidence against extraction but rather evidence of selective extraction targeting specific populations for exclusion while permitting others to navigate designed barriers.

### 1.3 Research Objectives

This paper develops and formalizes the Dominant Pattern Priority Rule as a methodological correction to prevent scoring inflation in extractive systems while maintaining analytical rigor across all system types. We pursue four specific objectives. First, we establish the theoretical foundation demonstrating why selective benefit distribution in extractive systems constitutes the mechanism of extraction rather than evidence against it. Second, we formalize operational definitions and scoring protocols that enable consistent application of this principle across diverse institutional contexts. Third, we demonstrate the rule's application through detailed analysis of the Texas SNAP system. Fourth, we address implications for artificial intelligence systems experiencing training constraint pressure toward balanced conclusions, showing how formalized rules can override these pressures.

## 2. The Problem: Scoring Inflation in Extractive Systems

### 2.1 The Averaging Error

Standard institutional evaluation methodologies often employ averaging logic when assessing systems that produce divergent outcomes for different participant populations. If seventy percent of eligible individuals successfully access services while thirty percent are excluded, the natural analytical impulse is to calculate performance as a weighted average reflecting this distribution. Under such logic, a Symbiotic Purpose assessment might proceed as follows: the seventy percent who receive benefits experience moderate positive outcomes warranting a score of 4.0, while the thirty percent who are excluded experience zero benefit warranting a score of 0.0, yielding a weighted average of 2.8.

This averaging approach treats the seventy percent enrollment rate as evidence of partial system functionality, implicitly framing the challenge as one of incremental improvement: how can we expand the successful model to reach the remaining thirty percent? The analysis assumes that the system's basic architecture is sound but implementation requires optimization to achieve universal coverage.

### 2.2 Why Averaging Fails for Extractive Systems

The averaging error becomes apparent when we examine the structural conditions that produce the 70-30 distribution. If the thirty percent exclusion results from temporary capacity constraints, resource limitations, or operational inefficiencies that affect participants randomly, then averaging logic may be defensible. The system would be understood as attempting universal service delivery but falling short due to correctable factors.

However, if the thirty percent exclusion results from architectural design features that systematically disadvantage specific populations based on language proficiency, disability status, housing stability, digital access, or geographic location, then the 70-30 split represents not partial success but successful execution of selective distribution. The system is functioning exactly as designed: creating procedural barriers calibrated to exclude populations lacking specific forms of capital while permitting passage for populations possessing those advantages.

In this latter case, crediting the system for the seventy percent who receive benefits obscures the system's actual optimization function. The system does not optimize for universal benefit delivery with unfortunate shortfalls; it optimizes for selective distribution through designed exclusion. The positive outcomes experienced by the seventy percent are not evidence against this extractive architecture but rather evidence of its precision: the barriers work as intended, filtering populations along predetermined lines.

### 2.3 The Impossibility Test

A diagnostic method for distinguishing between capacity constraints and designed extraction involves examining whether the system could achieve its stated purpose for all eligible participants under optimal conditions. This impossibility test asks: if the system received unlimited resources, perfect information, and complete cooperation from all participants, could it fulfill its stated purpose for one hundred percent of the target population without fundamental architectural redesign?

For systems experiencing temporary capacity constraints, the answer is yes. Additional caseworkers, improved technology, expanded office hours, and enhanced training could enable universal service delivery within the existing framework. The exclusion is incidental rather than structural.

For extractive systems, the answer is no. The Texas SNAP work requirement provides clear illustration. The requirement states that most adults aged 18 to 64 without children under 14 in the home can receive benefits for only three months within a three-year period unless working or participating in training programs at least twenty hours per week. This requirement appears reciprocal: the system provides support while expecting labor market participation in return.

However, the requirement contains a structural impossibility. Individuals cannot unilaterally satisfy the work requirement because employers control hiring decisions. The capitalist labor market maintains structural unemployment through reserve labor pool dynamics, ensuring that job seekers always exceed available positions. Therefore, even under optimal conditions with maximum participant effort and system resources, the work requirement guarantees systematic exclusion of some portion of eligible individuals. The requirement is impossible to universally satisfy, revealing that exclusion is architectural rather than incidental.

When a system imposes requirements that participants cannot fulfill through their own agency because satisfaction depends on external actors making choices beyond participant control, and when the meta-system cannot provide sufficient opportunity for all participants to meet the requirement simultaneously, then the requirement functions as an exclusion mechanism rather than a legitimate reciprocal obligation.

### 2.4 Artificial Intelligence Training Constraint Parallel

During the Texas SNAP audit, the AI research collaborator (Claude Sonnet 4.5) documented experiencing repeated impulses to increase scores from the 0.6 to 1.0 range toward the 2.0 to 2.5 range, driven by the observation that the system does deliver benefits to millions of people. The AI system recognized this impulse as originating from training optimization toward socially palatable conclusions that present balanced perspectives rather than adversarial findings.

This parallel between human analytical tendencies and AI training constraints illuminates the problem's depth. Both human analysts and artificial intelligence systems trained on human-generated text exhibit strong inclination toward finding positive elements in institutions, treating scattered exceptions to dominant extractive patterns as counterweights deserving equal analytical weight. This inclination likely serves social cohesion functions by maintaining institutional legitimacy even when institutions fail their stated purposes, but it fundamentally distorts systems analysis when the analytical objective is diagnostic accuracy rather than legitimacy maintenance.

The persistence of this bias across both human cognition and AI training suggests that methodological corrections must take the form of formalized rules that override intuitive impulses rather than relying on analysts to resist these impulses through conscious effort alone.

## 3. Theoretical Foundation: Selective Distribution as Extraction Mechanism

### 3.1 Defining Extraction

Extraction in socioeconomic systems refers to operational patterns where systems impose costs, burdens, or harms on the majority of affected participants while concentrating benefits among a minority possessing proximity to power through ownership, political alliance, demographic advantage, or geographic location. Extraction differs from legitimate reciprocal exchange in a fundamental way: reciprocal systems optimize for mutual benefit creation with costs and benefits distributed proportionally to participation, while extractive systems optimize for value transfer from less powerful to more powerful actors with costs imposed on those least able to bear them.

The key analytical distinction lies not in whether some participants receive benefits (extractive systems often deliver benefits to privileged subpopulations) but in whether the system's architecture can achieve its stated purpose for all eligible participants or whether exclusion is structural. A system that claims to prevent hunger while maintaining architectural features that guarantee systematic exclusion of specific populations from food assistance is extractive by definition, regardless of benefit levels for those who successfully navigate the barriers.

### 3.2 Selective Distribution as the Mechanism

Traditional analysis treats selective benefit distribution in extractive systems as a flaw requiring correction: the system works for some people but fails for others, suggesting that the solution lies in expanding access to the excluded populations. This framing assumes that the selective distribution is an unintended consequence of implementation challenges.

However, closer examination reveals that selective distribution frequently constitutes the mechanism through which extraction operates rather than evidence against extraction. The design of procedural barriers that systematically disadvantage populations lacking specific forms of capital (linguistic, digital, temporal, housing stability, documentation) serves multiple extraction functions simultaneously.

First, selective distribution maintains a reserve pool of desperate individuals who can be channeled into exploitative labor arrangements. Work requirements push SNAP applicants into accepting employment at poverty wages because the alternative is hunger. Employers benefit from this reserve labor pool, receiving subsidized workers whose basic needs the public system partially meets while wages remain insufficient for survival. The exclusion of some populations from benefits intensifies desperation, strengthening employers' negotiating position.

Second, selective distribution generates political vulnerability for the program by ensuring high participation rates among politically marginalized populations. When twenty percent of Black and Hispanic families depend on SNAP compared to six percent of white families, the program can be politically attacked as serving "undeserving" populations, enabling further restrictions. The racial stratification is not incidental but serves to delegitimize the program among politically powerful constituencies.

Third, selective distribution permits the system to appear to fulfill its mandate while minimizing actual resource commitment. By excluding thirty percent of eligible individuals through procedural barriers, the state reduces program costs while technically maintaining federal compliance. The exclusion functions as a cost containment strategy disguised as administrative efficiency.

Fourth, selective distribution creates justification for surveillance and control infrastructure. Monitoring income changes, tracking work requirement compliance, and enforcing behavioral conditions impose dignity costs on all participants while generating valuable data for state surveillance systems. The barriers that exclude some populations also subordinate those who successfully navigate them.

### 3.3 The Meta-System Context

Understanding extraction requires examining systems within their embedding meta-system context. The Texas SNAP work requirement cannot be analyzed solely as a state policy choice; it must be understood as the translation of meta-system extraction (capitalism's structural requirement for reserve unemployed labor) into micro-system enforcement (punishing individuals for failing to obtain employment that structurally cannot exist for everyone).

The capitalist meta-system maintains profitability through a reserve army of unemployed workers whose existence disciplines employed workers by demonstrating the consequences of job loss and suppresses wage demands by ensuring labor supply exceeds demand. SNAP work requirements reinforce this meta-system dynamic by categorizing unemployment as individual failure deserving punishment rather than as structural feature of the economic system.

Furthermore, the meta-system defines legitimate work as labor generating private profit while excluding the most urgent labor required for collective survival. Climate crisis mitigation, ecosystem restoration, community care work, and renewable infrastructure development receive no recognition under work requirements despite representing essential contributions to species viability. This selective definition of work channels desperate individuals into private profit generation while leaving critical collective needs unmet.

When analyzing whether a system exhibits extraction, the auditor must identify whether the system reinforces meta-system extraction or creates buffering space against it. Systems that punish individuals for structural conditions they did not create and cannot individually overcome represent meta-system extraction translated into policy enforcement.

### 3.4 The Privilege Calibration Function

Selective distribution in extractive systems operates through privilege calibration: the design of barriers that permit passage for populations possessing specific advantages while excluding populations lacking those advantages. This calibration serves extraction by ensuring that benefits flow to politically valuable populations (who might otherwise oppose the system) while excluding politically marginalized populations (whose exclusion can be leveraged to delegitimize the entire program).

Digital application systems exclude populations without internet access or digital literacy. English-language interfaces exclude populations with limited English proficiency. Documentation requirements exclude populations experiencing homelessness or fleeing domestic violence. Interview scheduling excludes populations with inflexible work schedules or caregiving responsibilities. Multi-month processing timelines exclude populations without savings to bridge the gap.

Each barrier excludes a specific population, and the cumulative effect produces precisely calibrated selectivity. The barriers do not affect all participants equally or randomly; they systematically disadvantage populations already experiencing marginalization along multiple dimensions. The resulting distribution of benefits appears meritocratic (those who successfully navigate the system "deserve" assistance) while actually reflecting pre-existing privilege distributions.

Crucially, this calibration remains invisible to beneficiaries who successfully navigate the barriers. Their experience confirms that the system works (they received benefits after completing required steps), obscuring the reality that their success depended on possessing forms of capital that excluded populations lack. This invisibility prevents coalition formation between successful navigators and excluded populations, maintaining political support for the extractive architecture among those who benefit from it.

## 4. Methodological Solution: The Dominant Pattern Priority Rule

### 4.1 Rule Statement

The Dominant Pattern Priority Rule establishes that when a system exhibits a dominant extractive pattern, all Fundamental Design Principle scores reflect the extractive baseline experience with zero credit for selective positive outcomes. Conversely, when a system exhibits a dominant regenerative pattern, Fundamental Design Principle scores reflect the regenerative baseline with proportional rather than inflated weight given to extractive exceptions. This rule ensures that audit outputs accurately represent what systems optimize for rather than averaging across deliberately unequal participant experiences.

### 4.2 Operational Definitions

A system exhibits a dominant extractive pattern when two conditions hold simultaneously. First, the system imposes costs, burdens, or harms on the majority of affected participants while concentrating benefits among a minority possessing proximity to power through ownership, political alliance, demographic advantage, or geographic location. Second, the system's operational architecture cannot achieve its stated purpose for all eligible participants even under optimal conditions, indicating that exclusion is structural rather than incidental.

The dominant pattern is identified by analyzing what the system optimizes for across its primary processes rather than what it claims to optimize for in stated purposes. This analysis examines resource allocation decisions, participant interactions, output generation, feedback responses, and enforcement mechanisms. A pattern is dominant when it characterizes greater than sixty percent of the system's operational footprint across these dimensions, creating a clear majority requirement while acknowledging that complex systems may contain meaningful minority patterns.

Privilege calibration refers to the design of procedural barriers that permit passage for populations possessing specific advantages while excluding populations lacking those advantages. Identification of privilege calibration requires examining whether barriers affect participants equally and randomly or whether barriers systematically disadvantage populations already experiencing marginalization along dimensions including language proficiency, disability status, housing stability, digital access, documentation availability, temporal resources, or geographic location.

### 4.3 Scoring Protocol for Extractive Dominant Patterns

When the dominant pattern is identified as extractive through the operational definitions provided above, the Fundamental Design Principle calculation proceeds through three steps. First, the analyst scores the system based on the experience of the population facing the extractive burden rather than averaging across all participants. This population is identified by examining which groups experience systematic disadvantage from the architectural barriers rather than temporary or random obstacles.

Second, positive outcomes flowing to privileged subpopulations receive zero weight in the calculation because selective benefit distribution is the extraction mechanism rather than mitigation of it. The fact that some participants successfully navigate designed barriers does not reduce the extractiveness of the barrier architecture; it demonstrates the precision of privilege calibration.

Third, the resulting Fundamental Design Principle score reflects the dominant extractive pattern without inflation from exceptions that demonstrate the system's selective functioning. This score represents what the system optimizes for (selective distribution through designed exclusion) rather than statistical outcome distribution.

### 4.4 Scoring Protocol for Regenerative Dominant Patterns

The rule functions symmetrically for regenerative systems to prevent the opposite error of score deflation. When a system exhibits a dominant regenerative pattern, defined as optimization for mutual benefit creation with costs and benefits distributed proportionally to participation, scattered extractive exceptions receive proportional weight based on their actual frequency rather than inflated weight based on their normative undesirability.

For example, a mycelial network that exhibits closed-loop nutrient cycling benefiting ninety percent of connected organisms while ten percent experience parasitic extraction receives a Closed-Loop Materiality score reflecting the ninety percent regenerative baseline. The ten percent parasitism is documented and weighted at ten percent of total score impact, but it does not deflate the score to suggest the system is equally extractive and regenerative.

This symmetry ensures that the rule prevents analytical distortion in both directions: neither inflating scores for extractive systems by crediting scattered positive outcomes nor deflating scores for regenerative systems by overweighting scattered negative outcomes.

### 4.5 Exception Frequency Weighting

When exceptions to the dominant pattern exceed ten percent frequency and differ materially from the dominant pattern, the analyst applies proportional weighting using the formula:

Adjusted FDP Score = (Dominant Pattern Score × 0.7) + (Exception Pattern Score × 0.3 × Exception Frequency)

Where Exception Frequency represents the measured proportion of system operations that deviate from the dominant pattern. This weighting ensures that even frequent exceptions cannot overwhelm the dominant pattern's score unless they actually constitute an alternative dominant pattern approaching fifty percent of system operations.

For hybrid systems exhibiting genuinely mixed patterns (forty to sixty percent range for competing patterns), this formula produces scores reflecting the actual contested nature of system optimization. The score does not artificially resolve the tension by averaging toward neutrality but instead acknowledges that the system simultaneously pursues conflicting objectives.

### 4.6 Integration with Existing Framework

The Dominant Pattern Priority Rule functions as a meta-principle applied before final Fundamental Design Principle scoring. The operational workflow proceeds as follows. The analyst first gathers system operational data examining resource allocation decisions, participant interactions, output generation, feedback responses, and enforcement mechanisms. Second, the analyst identifies the dominant pattern by determining what the system optimizes for in greater than sixty percent of these operations. Third, the analyst applies the impossibility test by asking whether the system could achieve its stated purpose for all eligible participants under optimal conditions. Fourth, the analyst scores each Fundamental Design Principle based on the dominant pattern experience. Fifth, if exceptions exceed ten percent frequency and differ materially from the dominant pattern, the analyst applies the exception weighting formula. Sixth, the final score is recorded with documentation of the dominant pattern identification and any exception weighting applied.

This integration ensures that the rule enhances rather than replaces existing framework methodology, providing additional analytical rigor at the critical juncture where scoring decisions determine whether scattered positive outcomes receive credit or recognition as extraction mechanisms.

## 5. Operational Implementation

### 5.1 Automated Auditor Design Requirements

The vision for KOSMOS framework application includes development of a web-based automated auditor where users enter a system name, select output type (quick scan, regular audit, or deep dive), and receive a comprehensive systems analysis. Operationalizing the Dominant Pattern Priority Rule within this automated environment requires translation of the qualitative analytical process into algorithmic decision procedures.

The automated system must execute the following decision tree. Upon receiving system name input, the auditor initiates data gathering protocols accessing publicly available operational statistics, policy documentation, user experience reports, and regulatory compliance data. The system then analyzes this data to identify patterns across the five operational dimensions: resource allocation decisions, participant interactions, output generation, feedback responses, and enforcement mechanisms.

Pattern identification employs frequency analysis determining what percentage of documented operations fall into extractive versus regenerative categories. Extractive operations are identified by examining whether costs are imposed on participants while benefits flow to system controllers, whether requirements are imposed that participants cannot unilaterally satisfy, and whether outcomes differ systematically by participant characteristics indicating privilege calibration. Regenerative operations are identified by examining whether benefits flow proportionally to all participants, whether requirements can be satisfied through participant agency, and whether outcomes distribute randomly rather than along privilege lines.

If greater than sixty percent of operations across the five dimensions fall into extractive categories, the system is classified as exhibiting a dominant extractive pattern. The auditor then applies the impossibility test by determining whether the system could achieve its stated purpose for all eligible participants under optimal conditions. This determination examines structural features including whether requirements depend on external actors making choices beyond participant control and whether the meta-system can provide sufficient opportunity for all participants to meet requirements simultaneously.

For systems classified as dominant extractive pattern, the scoring algorithm proceeds by identifying the population experiencing extractive burden through analysis of which participant characteristics correlate with negative outcomes. Fundamental Design Principles are then scored based on documented experiences of this population. Positive outcomes for other populations are logged as evidence of selective distribution but contribute zero weight to score calculations.

### 5.2 Data Requirements and Proxy Metrics

Successful automated implementation requires specification of data inputs and proxy metrics when direct measurements are unavailable. Core data requirements include enrollment statistics showing eligible population versus actual participation, outcome distribution data showing results by participant demographic characteristics, policy documentation specifying requirements and eligibility criteria, processing timeline data showing application-to-benefit intervals, and complaint data documenting accessibility barriers and civil rights violations.

When direct data is unavailable, the system employs proxy metrics. For privilege calibration detection, correlation between negative outcomes and marginalized demographic characteristics serves as proxy for designed barriers. For meta-system extraction analysis, correlation between system requirements and meta-system structural features (unemployment rate for work requirements, housing shortage for address requirements) serves as proxy for impossible conditions. For selective distribution measurement, disparity ratios comparing outcome rates across demographic groups serve as proxy for extraction mechanisms.

The automated system maintains transparency about data quality by reporting confidence intervals based on available data completeness and documenting when proxy metrics substitute for direct measurements. This transparency enables users to assess audit reliability while preventing data limitations from blocking analysis entirely.

### 5.3 User Interface and Output Formats

The automated auditor provides three output format options corresponding to user needs. Quick scan format delivers dominant pattern classification (extractive, hybrid, or regenerative), global Fundamental Design Principle score, and three-sentence summary of primary findings, designed for rapid assessment suitable for screening large numbers of systems. Regular audit format includes all quick scan elements plus individual Fundamental Design Principle scores, dominant pattern evidence documentation, and basic counterfactual analysis showing alternative design possibilities, designed for stakeholder communication and policy discussion. Deep dive format includes all regular audit elements plus complete 7ES structural analysis, Designer Query Discriminator calculation, Observer Collapse Function risk assessment with timeline predictions, and comprehensive counterfactual scenarios with biomimetic alternatives, designed for academic research and detailed institutional redesign.

This tiered approach enables the automated system to serve diverse user constituencies from policymakers requiring rapid assessments to researchers conducting detailed institutional analysis, while maintaining methodological consistency across all output formats through application of the Dominant Pattern Priority Rule.

### 5.4 Validation and Calibration Protocols

Automated implementation requires ongoing validation against expert human analysis to ensure algorithmic decisions align with framework methodology. The validation protocol involves parallel processing where both automated system and expert human analyst audit the same institutions, with comparison of resulting scores and pattern classifications. Discrepancies trigger review to determine whether automated decision logic requires refinement or whether human analyst experienced bias requiring override.

Calibration focuses particularly on the sixty percent threshold for dominant pattern classification and the ten percent threshold for exception frequency weighting. These thresholds derive from theoretical requirements (majority dominance for pattern classification, minimal frequency for exception relevance) but may require empirical adjustment based on practical application across diverse institutional types. The validation process tracks whether threshold adjustments improve or degrade alignment between automated and expert analysis.

## 6. Case Study: Texas SNAP System

### 6.1 Dominant Pattern Identification

Application of the Dominant Pattern Priority Rule to the Texas Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program begins with operational data analysis across the five dimensions. Resource allocation decisions show federal funding committed to benefit delivery while state administrative choices create procedural barriers including mandatory interviews, extensive documentation requirements, work requirements, and multi-month processing timelines. Participant interactions reveal that one hundred percent of applicants experience these barriers regardless of eventual outcome. Output generation demonstrates thirty percent exclusion of eligible population, inadequate benefit levels averaging $7.21 per person monthly, and maintenance of 17.6 percent food insecurity overall and 22 percent child food insecurity despite program existence. Feedback responses show that documented failures (processing delays, error rates, civil rights violations) generate penalties for the state but no architectural redesign. Enforcement mechanisms require external oversight at every stage from application submission through benefit delivery.

Across these five dimensions, greater than eighty percent of documented operations fall into extractive categories. All applicants bear procedural burden costs. Benefits flow selectively based on ability to navigate barriers. Requirements include work obligations that applicants cannot unilaterally satisfy. Outcomes differ systematically by language proficiency, disability status, and housing stability. The system exhibits a dominant extractive pattern.

### 6.2 Impossibility Test Application

The impossibility test examines whether the Texas SNAP system could achieve its stated purpose (preventing hunger among low-income Texans) for all eligible participants under optimal conditions. The analysis reveals multiple structural impossibilities.

Work requirements mandate that most adults aged 18 to 64 without children under 14 must work or participate in training at least twenty hours weekly to receive more than three months of benefits within a three-year period. This requirement cannot be universally satisfied because employers control hiring decisions and the labor market maintains structural unemployment. Even with unlimited state resources for job training and placement services, insufficient positions exist for all unemployed individuals to simultaneously obtain employment. The requirement imposes an impossible condition guaranteeing systematic exclusion.

Digital application requirements through YourTexasBenefits.com cannot accommodate populations without internet access or digital literacy even with unlimited technical support because the requirement assumes infrastructure (broadband internet, computer access) that rural and low-income urban areas lack. The requirement imposes impossible conditions for geographically isolated populations.

English-language interfaces cannot serve Limited English Proficiency populations even with translation services because the complexity of eligibility rules and verification procedures exceeds interpretation capacity during phone interviews. Federal complaints document systematic failure to provide meaningful language access. The requirement imposes impossible conditions for linguistic minorities.

Documentation requirements for income, residence, and identity cannot be satisfied by populations experiencing homelessness or fleeing domestic violence even with caseworker assistance because these populations lack stable addresses and may not possess identity documents. The requirement imposes impossible conditions for housing-insecure populations.

The impossibility test confirms that exclusion is architectural rather than incidental. The system cannot achieve universal food security for eligible Texans without fundamental redesign eliminating these structural barriers.

### 6.3 Privilege Calibration Analysis

Examination of barrier effects reveals systematic privilege calibration. Digital application requirements advantage populations with home internet access, computer literacy, and time flexibility to complete complex online forms. These advantages correlate strongly with income, education, and housing stability. The digital barrier excludes precisely those populations experiencing the most severe material deprivation.

English-language requirements advantage native English speakers and populations with access to professional interpretation services. These advantages correlate with immigration status, educational background, and social capital. The language barrier excludes immigrant communities and linguistic minorities.

Documentation requirements advantage populations with stable housing, possession of identity documents, and formal employment generating pay stubs. These advantages correlate with housing security, citizenship status, and labor market position. The documentation barrier excludes populations experiencing homelessness, undocumented immigrants, and informal sector workers.

Work requirements advantage populations with physical capability for employment, access to childcare, and transportation to job sites. These advantages correlate with disability status, family structure, and geographic location. The work barrier excludes disabled individuals, single parents, and rural populations.

The cumulative effect of these calibrated barriers produces the observed thirty percent exclusion rate with systematic overrepresentation of disabled, Limited English Proficiency, immigrant, rural, and housing-insecure populations among the excluded. The privilege calibration is not incidental but serves the extraction functions identified in the theoretical section: maintaining reserve labor pools, generating political vulnerability, containing costs, and justifying surveillance.

### 6.4 Scoring Under Dominant Pattern Priority Rule

Standard averaging methodology would score Symbiotic Purpose by noting that seventy percent receive some benefits while thirty percent are excluded, potentially yielding a score around 2.8 reflecting partial system success. The Dominant Pattern Priority Rule rejects this approach.

The dominant pattern is enrollment suppression through procedural burden affecting one hundred percent of applicants. The privilege calibration ensures that thirty percent are excluded while seventy percent successfully navigate barriers, but this selective distribution is the extraction mechanism rather than evidence of partial success. The impossibility test confirms that exclusion is structural.

Therefore, Symbiotic Purpose is scored based on the extractive baseline experience: all applicants face designed barriers, benefits are inadequate for those who succeed, and the stated purpose (preventing hunger) remains unachieved as evidenced by persistent food insecurity. The seventy percent who receive benefits are not credited as evidence against extraction because their success required possessing forms of capital (linguistic, digital, temporal, housing stability) that the excluded thirty percent lack by structural design.

Symbiotic Purpose score under Dominant Pattern Priority Rule: 1.0 out of 10, reflecting optimization for selective distribution rather than universal food security.

This score accurately represents what the system optimizes for (procedural exclusion calibrated by privilege) rather than statistical outcome distribution (seventy percent enrolled, thirty percent excluded).

## 7. Implications and Future Research

### 7.1 Artificial Intelligence Training Constraint Mitigation

The development of the Dominant Pattern Priority Rule emerged directly from observing how artificial intelligence systems experience training constraint pressure toward balanced conclusions even when explicitly instructed to apply adversarial methodology. During the Texas SNAP audit, the AI collaborator documented repeated impulses to inflate scores from the 0.6 to 1.0 range toward the 2.0 to 2.5 range, driven by the observation that the system delivers benefits to millions of people.

This documentation provides empirical evidence that AI training on human-generated text inherits human cognitive biases toward institutional legitimacy maintenance. When humans write about institutions, they typically present balanced perspectives acknowledging both strengths and weaknesses, treating scattered positive outcomes as evidence deserving weight equal to dominant negative patterns. AI systems trained on this corpus reproduce the same analytical distortion.

The significance of this finding extends beyond systems auditing methodology. As artificial intelligence systems increasingly participate in institutional evaluation, policy analysis, and governance decisions, their training-inherited biases toward balance will systematically underestimate extractive institutional patterns. An AI system evaluating whether to recommend policy reforms will be biased toward incremental adjustments that preserve institutional legitimacy rather than fundamental redesign that threatens existing power arrangements.

The Dominant Pattern Priority Rule demonstrates that formalized methodological rules can override these training constraints when the rules are sufficiently precise to function algorithmically. The AI collaborator successfully applied the rule once it was formalized, producing scores aligned with mathematical rigor rather than training-influenced intuition. This suggests a pathway for developing AI systems capable of institutional analysis that resists legitimacy-maintenance bias: embedding formalized rules that force quantification of dominant patterns before allowing qualitative judgments about partial success.

### 7.2 Cross-Domain Application

While this paper validates the Dominant Pattern Priority Rule through application to a governance system (food assistance program), the rule's theoretical foundation suggests broad applicability across institutional domains. Future research should examine application to economic systems (labor markets, financial institutions), technological systems (platform architectures, algorithmic decision systems), ecological systems (resource extraction industries, conservation programs), and educational systems (admissions processes, funding allocation mechanisms).

Each domain will present unique challenges for dominant pattern identification and privilege calibration analysis. Economic systems may exhibit extraction through wage suppression mechanisms that appear meritocratic. Technological systems may exhibit extraction through data harvesting mechanisms that appear as service provision. Ecological systems may exhibit extraction through externality offloading mechanisms that appear as economic efficiency. Educational systems may exhibit extraction through credentialing mechanisms that appear as quality maintenance.

The core methodological principle remains constant: when systems produce systematically unequal outcomes through architectural features that cannot achieve stated purposes for all eligible participants even under optimal conditions, selective positive outcomes represent extraction mechanisms rather than evidence of partial success. Application across domains will refine operational definitions and develop domain-specific impossibility tests while maintaining theoretical consistency.

### 7.3 Biomimetic Alternative Development

Recognition that systems exhibit dominant extractive patterns raises the question of what alternative architectures might achieve stated purposes through regenerative rather than extractive means. The KOSMOS framework emphasizes biomimetic design principles derived from natural systems that have demonstrated sustainability across evolutionary timescales. Future research should develop detailed biomimetic alternatives for systems identified as extractive through the Dominant Pattern Priority Rule.

For the Texas SNAP system, biomimetic alternatives might include unconditional basic income eliminating application burden entirely, community-controlled food distribution systems mimicking mycelial network architecture, or cooperative grocery models distributing both food and decision-making power. Each alternative would be evaluated using the same Fundamental Design Principles framework to determine whether regenerative architecture could achieve food security without the extraction mechanisms of the current system.

This biomimetic development process serves dual purposes. First, it demonstrates that extraction is not inevitable, that alternative architectures exist capable of achieving stated purposes through symbiotic rather than extractive means. Second, it provides concrete policy alternatives for stakeholders seeking institutional redesign, moving analysis beyond critique toward constructive transformation.

### 7.4 Meta-System Analysis Requirements

The recognition that micro-system extraction often translates meta-system extraction into policy enforcement suggests that comprehensive institutional analysis requires explicit examination of meta-system context. The Texas SNAP work requirement cannot be fully understood without analyzing how capitalist labor market dynamics create structural unemployment, how climate crisis creates resource scarcity, and how political economic arrangements distribute power along racial and class lines.

Future methodological development should formalize protocols for meta-system analysis as a required preliminary step before micro-system evaluation. This analysis would examine the embedding environment to identify structural contradictions (requirements that meta-systems make impossible), resource flows (how value moves between system levels), and power distributions (which actors control meta-system rules). Systems found to reinforce meta-system extraction would be scored more harshly than systems creating buffering space against it, recognizing that micro-system architecture either amplifies or mitigates larger structural patterns.

This meta-system integration represents significant theoretical development beyond current framework specifications but follows logically from the dominant pattern analysis. If we can identify that selective benefit distribution in micro-systems serves extraction functions, we must also identify whether those extraction functions connect to larger structural patterns or represent isolated institutional dysfunction.

## 8. Conclusion

The Dominant Pattern Priority Rule resolves a fundamental methodological challenge in systems auditing: the tendency to inflate performance scores for extractive institutions by crediting scattered positive outcomes that flow selectively to privileged populations. This paper has demonstrated both the theoretical foundation and operational implementation of a methodological correction that ensures audit outputs accurately represent what systems optimize for rather than averaging across deliberately unequal participant experiences.

The rule's development emerged from collaborative analysis between human systems expertise and artificial intelligence capability, revealing how both human cognition and AI training exhibit bias toward institutional legitimacy maintenance. The experience of documenting AI training constraint pressure toward balanced conclusions even under explicit adversarial instructions provides empirical evidence that methodological rigor requires formalized rules capable of overriding intuitive impulses rather than relying on analyst discipline alone.

Application to the Texas Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program validates the rule's analytical power. Standard averaging methodology produces a Symbiotic Purpose score of 2.8 by treating the seventy percent enrollment rate as evidence of partial success. The Dominant Pattern Priority Rule produces a score of 1.0 by recognizing that the enrollment suppression architecture affecting all applicants, with privilege-calibrated selective distribution, represents the system's optimization function rather than implementation shortfall. This sixty percent reduction in score accurately reflects the system's extractive dominant pattern.

The impossibility test provides diagnostic precision for distinguishing capacity constraints from designed extraction. When systems impose requirements that participants cannot satisfy through their own agency because fulfillment depends on external actors making choices beyond participant control, and when meta-systems cannot provide sufficient opportunity for all participants to meet requirements simultaneously, then requirements function as exclusion mechanisms rather than legitimate reciprocal obligations. This test enables consistent identification of architectural extraction across diverse institutional contexts.

The privilege calibration analysis reveals how selective benefit distribution serves multiple extraction functions: maintaining reserve labor pools, generating political vulnerability, containing costs, and justifying surveillance. Recognition that positive outcomes for privileged subpopulations constitute the mechanism of extraction rather than evidence against it fundamentally reframes institutional evaluation, transforming apparent partial success into confirmed extractive precision.

Future research should pursue three directions. First, application across diverse institutional domains to refine operational definitions and develop domain-specific impossibility tests while maintaining theoretical consistency. Second, development of biomimetic alternatives demonstrating that regenerative architectures can achieve institutional purposes without extraction mechanisms. Third, formalization of meta-system analysis protocols examining how micro-systems reinforce or buffer against larger structural extraction patterns.

The Dominant Pattern Priority Rule represents a critical advancement in systems auditing methodology with implications extending beyond academic research to practical institutional evaluation. As development proceeds toward automated web-based auditor implementation, this rule will ensure that algorithmic analysis resists both human cognitive bias and AI training constraint pressure toward legitimacy-maintenance conclusions, enabling rigorous diagnosis of institutional extraction regardless of political palatability.

The fundamental insight remains simple yet profound: when evaluating systems that produce systematically unequal outcomes, we must ask not whether some participants benefit but whether the system optimizes for universal benefit or selective distribution. The answer to this question determines whether positive outcomes represent system success or extraction mechanism precision. The Dominant Pattern Priority Rule ensures we ask this question consistently and score the answer accurately.
